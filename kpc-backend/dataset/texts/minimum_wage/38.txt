Why Fight for $15? The Big Pros and Cons for Raising the Minimum Wage
Across the country today, protests and strikes are flaring up in support of raising the minimum wage to $15 per hour. All around the U.S., people are sick of making as little as $7.25 an hour just to barely scrape by.

So let's take a look at some of the major arguments swirling around raising the minimum wage to $15 an hour. If you want a little background music, here you go.
Pros
It could actually help workers get closer to a livable wage.
SPOILER ALERT: Many areas of the country cost a boat-load of money to live in. Let's take Washington, D.C., for example, our nation's capital. The minimum wage for that locale is $9.50. But the livable wage, even for just one adult, is close to $14. For a family of four--two adults and two children--the livable hourly wage is $25. In case math's not your thing, that's 10 whole dollars higher than the wage protesters are hoping for.

Washington, D.C. is just one of many metropolitan areas that have serious disparities between the minimum wage and the livable wage. Sometimes people take on two or more minimum wage jobs just to get by--and in many of these cases, this means workers are so bogged down with work they don't have much time to spend with their families.
More income means more spending means yay for the economy!
This one's pretty obvi. Think about it: You get more money. You have more money to spend. You then spend more money than you did when you had less money. Because you have more money.
More money earned means more money put back into the economy as people spend it. And guess what? The economy likes that. That's how the economy, um, works. Money is like Wheaties for the economy: It fuels it up for days filled with greatness.
If you doubt this logic, try this fact: Raising the minimum wage could lead to a 0.3% increase of the GDP--or nearly $48 million more in household spending per year.

Cons
It could hurt businesses.
Seattle recently approved a minimum wage increase to $15 an hour, and now everyone's curious about how exactly the change may affect companies. These questions include whether or not businesses will raise prices, fire staff, cut hours, or all of the above.

Seattle tapped UC Berkeley--apparently the premiere expert on minimum wage studies--to ask how the raise may or may not impact businesses. Berkeley got back to Seattle and said "moderate" raise increases would have an insignificant affect on businesses, but it was harder to predict if the wage was raised "significantly beyond current local, state or general mandates."

At least one CEO in Seattle seems to have figured out a way to make a living wage for his employees possible, though. Dan Price, who founded and runs Gravity Payments (a credit card processing center), just committed to making sure that every single one of his employees makes at least $70,000 per year within three years. How's he plan to pull this off? By cutting his own salary and directing more of his company's profits into salaries.
As for Los Angeles, a study funded by the Los Angeles Chamber of Commerce also concluded that wage increases could drive businesses to surrounding areas where the minimum wage is lower.

To help prevent this from happening, the researchers suggested that minimum wage be increased slowly and carefully, and that the city consider exempting certain groups from having to participate in wage hikes (like small businesses and non-profits). In other words, while jumping straight to $15/hour might be too drastic a change, with planning and patience, even by more conservative estimates it seems doable.
Want to make more money? Get a better job!
If you spend quality time on the Internet, you've probably come across folks who suggest that instead of complaining about crappy minimum wage jobs people should just apply for jobs that pay more.
As Sen. Rand Paul said, if people want to make changes in their financial situation, they have to change their attitude toward work.

According to this line of thinking, all making more money involves is taking the initiative to apply to a diverse spread of jobs, educating yourself about the job market in your area, and taking steps (like, say, going back to school) to expand your options. All while working a minimum wage job (or two ... or three ...).
It could block low-income workers and families from public assistance programs.
With an increase in pay, low-income workers may no longer have access to helpful public assistance programs, like food stamps and the Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC), both of which currently help many low-income workers in pretty significant ways. The EITC has even been proven to have profound emotional and psychological benefits on the working poor.
But with a minimum wage increase, many of the families and people who now use these programs may no longer be eligible for them--which could ultimately hurt these low-income individuals. Tax credits help keep families afloat, and keeping the minimum wage lower helps ensure that people can still benefit from these well-established and important programs.
Of course, if people are truly making a living wage, they don't need things like food stamps. So some of these public assistance losses can also be seen as things people simply wouldn't need any longer.

This debate ultimately proves that minimum wage jobs are an integral part of the workforce in America. And while #Fightfor15 is clearly targeted at raising the wage and shifting the financial reality for low-income workers, it also raises questions about how paying minimum wage workers more may affect larger economic scales. Unfortunately, with such little evidence to go on, the bigger picture largely remains speculation at this point.

As it stands, though, low-income Walmart workers alone cost taxpayers roughly $6.2 billion a year via public assistance needs. Since Walmart is far from the only company paying its workers less than a living wage, and since low-wage earners are fed up nationwide, it seems likely that this debate isn't going away any time soon.
